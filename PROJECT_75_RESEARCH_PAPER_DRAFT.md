## Abstract

**Background:** Segmentation errors can propagate into tetrahedral meshes and
compromise their use in finite-element (FE) workflows. This study developed a
reproducible synthetic CT-to-mesh pipeline and investigated whether a
GraphSAGE-style graph neural network (GNN) could guide local mesh smoothing.

**Methods:** A procedural constructive-solid-geometry phantom library was
generated at high resolution, with clean reference surfaces and three
degradation tiers. Each tiered volume was segmented, converted to a surface
using marching cubes, repaired, and tetrahedralized with TetGen. Tetrahedra
were represented as nodes in a face-adjacency graph and described by 15
geometric and neighborhood features. A residual GraphSAGE model produced a
per-element correction logit and smoothing-strength prediction. Training
labels were generated by applying a shared quality-guarded local smoothing
operator. The model was trained using focal classification loss and masked
regression loss. Validation calibration searched four probability thresholds
and imposed constraints on modified-element fraction and minimum scaled
Jacobian.

**Results:** The saved checkpoint (best epoch 138) reported validation
element-label F1 0.885, precision 0.795, recall 0.998, average precision
0.903, and positive-only regression MAE 0.109. In a reduced, tier-balanced
100-correction-operation calibration, no threshold satisfied both eligibility
constraints. An explicitly exploratory evaluation on three validation
phantoms (nine phantom-tier meshes) at threshold 0.2 showed a median poor
element percentage of 6.00% for GNN correction versus 13.55% for the
classical baseline, but median minimum scaled Jacobian was lower (0.00571
versus 0.01255). FE checks converged for all nine meshes in both arms. Median
runtime was 18.25 seconds for the GNN arm and 3.50 seconds for the classical
arm. All three exploratory phantoms had also appeared in the calibration
search. In an archived, pre-consolidation benchmark on 23 phantom IDs and
three tiers, the historical GNN arm had a median paired increase of 1.347
percentage points in poor-element fraction versus classical, despite a
higher minimum scaled Jacobian; this benchmark used an earlier method and the
same IDs now designated as the test split.

**Conclusion:** The work demonstrates a complete synthetic pipeline and a
GNN that predicts geometric correction labels well on validation meshes.
However, classifier metrics did not guarantee safe or efficient mesh
correction. The current threshold failed the validation gates, the exploratory
sample was small and reused for selection, and correction added substantial
runtime. An independent confirmatory evaluation is not available. The present
evidence supports a methods and feasibility report, not a claim that the
current GNN improves simulation readiness.

## 1. Introduction

Conversion of volumetric imaging into a simulation-ready finite-element mesh
is a sequence of coupled operations. Segmentation determines the boundary
surface; surface discretization and repair influence the tetrahedral mesh; and
tetrahedral geometry influences numerical conditioning and solver behavior.
Poorly shaped elements can be undesirable even when a mesh is topologically
valid and a particular solver happens to converge.

The Project 75 specification proposes an integrated workflow for testing
whether one learned component can improve that process. The focus is not a new
meshing algorithm, but the integration of procedural data generation,
classical surface-to-volume meshing, graph-based element prediction, local
correction, and a downstream FE sanity check. Since ground-truth CT data are
not part of this study, all data are synthetic and provide a controlled
reference geometry.

The research question is:

> Given a classical tetrahedral mesh generated from a tiered synthetic volume,
> can a GNN identify regions for a shared quality-guarded local smoother so
> that mesh quality and FE readiness improve without unacceptable geometric
> distortion or computational overhead?

This manuscript distinguishes three different kinds of evidence:

1. **Predictor validation:** whether the GNN predicts correction-derived
   element labels and smoothing targets.
2. **Operational calibration:** whether a thresholded prediction policy
   satisfies pre-specified correction-safety and modification constraints.
3. **Mesh-outcome evaluation:** whether applying the policy changes quality,
   geometry, FE behavior, and runtime relative to paired baselines.

These are not interchangeable. High classification F1 or recall alone cannot
establish that a correction policy improves a mesh.

## 2. Materials and methods

### 2.1 Procedural phantom library

The library generator creates independently parameterized CSG density
phantoms. Parameters include a geometry seed, body scale, rib thickness, and
internal channel radius. Each candidate is checked before acceptance for
non-negative density, a single connected foreground object, no contact with
the volume boundary, and a head-mask volume within 0.6–1.4 times a nominal
volume.

The configured high-resolution grid is \(256^3\). A clean reference surface
is extracted from the high-resolution density before the degradation
procedure. The volume is then reduced to \(128^3\) by averaging each
\(2 \times 2 \times 2\) voxel block. For moderate and poor tiers, Gaussian
blur and additive zero-mean Gaussian noise are applied before segmentation.
The segmentation threshold is stored in the manifest and applied by the
classical pipeline.

| Input tier | Blur parameter | Effective blur sigma on the high-resolution grid | Noise standard deviation | Segmentation threshold |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Clean | 0 | 0 | 0.00 | 0.5 |
| Moderate | 1 | 2 | 0.05 | 0.5 |
| Poor | 2 | 4 | 0.10 | 0.7 |

The blur sigma is multiplied by two because the high-resolution and degraded
grids differ by a factor of two along each axis.

The configured library contains 150 phantom instances, with fixed splits by
phantom ID:

| Split | Phantoms | Tiered volume-mesh cases |
|---|---:|---:|
| Train | 105 | 315 |
| Validation | 22 | 66 |
| Test | 23 | 69 |
| Total | 150 | 450 |

All three tiers for one phantom remain in the same split, preventing direct
cross-split leakage of nearly identical geometry. A seed and sampled geometry
specification are stored per phantom. The manifest maps each `(phantom ID,
tier)` pair to its volume, segmentation threshold, and clean reference.

### 2.2 Classical segmentation-to-tetrahedral-mesh pipeline

The baseline pipeline was:

1. Threshold the degraded volume.
2. Fill enclosed binary-mask holes and retain the largest connected component.
3. Pad the mask and extract a triangulated isosurface with marching cubes.
4. Repair the surface by merging vertices, removing degenerate/duplicate and
   broken faces, filling holes, and fixing normals.
5. Tetrahedralize the repaired surface with default TetGen settings.
6. Save the classical `.vtu` mesh and record quality measures and timing for
   segmentation, extraction, repair, and tetrahedralization.

The uncorrected classical mesh is the shared paired baseline for subsequent
method comparisons. Re-tetrahedralized methods may change the element count;
therefore, percentages must be interpreted together with absolute counts and
element growth.

### 2.3 Element graph, features, and training labels

Each tetrahedron is a graph node. An undirected adjacency exists when two
tetrahedra share a triangular face with exactly two owners. Boundary faces and
non-manifold faces with more than two owners do not form dual-graph edges.
The adjacency is passed to the GNN in both directions.

The implementation computes 15 element features:

1. scaled Jacobian;
2. normalized edge ratio;
3. log absolute volume;
4. log maximum edge length;
5. log minimum edge length;
6. log root-mean-square edge length;
7–8. minimum and maximum dihedral angles;
9–11. neighboring mean, minimum, and maximum scaled Jacobian;
12. dual-graph distance to a boundary element;
13. element-volume ratio to neighborhood mean;
14. edge-length ratio to neighborhood mean; and
15. boundary-contact indicator.

The scaled Jacobian is computed geometrically at all four tetrahedron corners,
normalized by the product of corner edge lengths, and clipped to \([-1,1]\).
The implementation treats an element as poor before correction when its
scaled Jacobian is below 0.25 or its signed volume is at or below a small
positive tolerance.

Labels are generated using the same shared `smart_smooth` operator used by
the targeted correction methods. Vertices incident to poor elements are
considered as candidates. A poor element receives a positive classification
label only if smoothing improves its scaled Jacobian and its resulting signed
volume remains positive. The regression target is the mean accepted vertex
smoothing strength for that element. Regression loss is evaluated only on
positive elements. This creates internally consistent labels but also makes
the prediction target dependent on the chosen operator and its acceptance
settings.

The shared correction configuration is:

| Setting | Value |
|---|---|
| Operator | `smart_smooth` |
| Boundary handling | Enabled with tangential movement constraints |
| Acceptance objective | Penalty |
| Target scaled Jacobian | 0.30 |
| Minimum local floor ratio | 0.5 |
| Smoothing cycles | 8 |

### 2.4 GNN architecture and training

The model is a residual GraphSAGE-style network [1]. A feature encoder maps
the 15 input features to a 64-dimensional hidden representation. Three
residual message-passing blocks use mean-aggregating `SAGEConv`, layer
normalization, GELU, dropout, and a feed-forward residual sub-block. Separate
element-level heads produce a correction logit and a sigmoid-bounded
smoothing-strength value in \([0,1]\).

| Training parameter | Setting |
|---|---:|
| Maximum epochs | 150 |
| Optimizer | Adam |
| Learning rate | \(10^{-3}\) |
| Weight decay | \(10^{-4}\) |
| Graph batch size | 2 |
| Classification loss | Focal binary cross-entropy, alpha 0.75, gamma 2 |
| Regression loss | MSE masked to positive labels |
| Combined objective | Classification loss + 2.0 × regression loss |
| Gradient norm clipping | 1.0 |
| Early stopping patience | 20 epochs without validation AP improvement |
| Random seed | 0 |

Feature normalization is calculated from training graphs only. Uniform
rescaling augmentation is sampled from 0.9–1.1 and updates the log-volume and
log-edge-length features consistently. The model checkpoint is selected by
validation average precision, not by the final epoch.

The saved checkpoint was selected at epoch 138. Its validation metrics were:

| Predictor metric | Value |
|---|---:|
| F1 at the training-time threshold of 0.5 | 0.8850 |
| Precision | 0.7948 |
| Recall | 0.9984 |
| Average precision | 0.9031 |
| Positive-only regression MAE | 0.1087 |
| Validation loss | 0.0431 |

These metrics measure prediction of correction-derived element labels on
validation graphs. They are not mesh-improvement metrics and are not the
calibrated operating-point F1.

### 2.5 GNN inference and correction operator

At inference, predictions are restricted to a local region formed by poor
elements and up to two dual-graph neighbor hops. Elements with probability
above the selected threshold are flagged. Vertices incident to flagged
elements are assigned a movement mask, and predicted strengths are aggregated
over incident flagged elements.

The shared smoother uses inverse-edge-length-weighted adjacency, a greedy
vertex coloring, and local proposals. Boundary movement is projected into the
boundary tangent plane and limited by a displacement tolerance. Candidate
moves are checked against incident tetrahedra. The configured penalty-based
acceptance condition discourages increasing the count of poor incident
elements, reducing the local quality penalty, and driving any local minimum
scaled Jacobian below a fraction of its pre-move value.

The GNN changes the targeting and strength inputs to this smoother; it does
not change the underlying smoothing operator. The `rule_targeted` arm targets
elements based on the geometric poor-element criterion, while the `gnn` arm
uses the predicted flags and strengths.

### 2.6 Calibration protocol

Calibration uses validation data only. It estimates a scalar regression
strength calibration and evaluates probability thresholds \(\{0.2,0.3,0.4,
0.5\}\) using the actual correction operator. A threshold is eligible only if
both conditions hold:

1. the median modified-element fraction of the GNN arm is lower than that of
   the rule-targeted arm; and
2. the median minimum scaled Jacobian is at least 90% of the classical
   median minimum scaled Jacobian.

To reduce runtime, the completed reduced calibration evaluated 20
deterministically selected validation meshes, balanced across the three
tiers, with five correction runs per mesh (one rule-targeted and four GNN
thresholds), for 100 correction operations. Validation prediction inference
used all 66 validation meshes. The sample is appropriate only as a reduced
calibration search, not as a substitute for complete validation or test
evaluation.

### 2.7 Final evaluation metrics and FE sanity check

The implemented evaluation methods are `classical`, `global_taubin`,
`global_taubin_retet`, `blind_global_smart`, `rule_targeted`, `gnn`, and
`retet_global_quality`. The evaluator records:

- poor-element count, percentage, and count change;
- inverted-element fraction;
- minimum, mean, and first-percentile scaled Jacobian;
- Hausdorff/chamfer surface-distance statistics to the clean reference;
- volume error, watertightness, and tetrahedron growth;
- flagged elements, modified elements, moved points, and fallback status;
- FE assembly/solver success, iterations, residual, and timings; and
- classical, feature, GNN inference, smoothing, verification, FE, and total
  runtimes.

The FE check assembles a linear-tetrahedron Poisson/heat-conduction stiffness
system and solves a Dirichlet problem. Inverted or degenerate tetrahedra cause
assembly failure rather than being dropped. This is a solver-readiness proxy,
not a full structural, thermal, or CFD validation.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Historical, pre-consolidation benchmark

The archived `benchmark_long.csv` contains 552 successful method rows:
23 phantom IDs × three tiers × eight methods. The historical IDs exactly
match the 23 IDs currently listed in the test split. The method set contains
legacy arms (`classical_cond`, `global_smart`, `global_smart_boundary`, and
the historical `gnn`) that are not identical to the consolidated current
method set. The CSV does not preserve all calibration and label-version
information needed to establish equivalence to the current implementation.

In the archived paired comparison of the historical GNN with the historical
classical arm across 69 phantom-tier cases:

| Outcome | Historical classical median | Historical GNN median | Paired median GNN − classical |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Poor-element percentage | 13.690% | 14.687% | +1.347 percentage points |
| Minimum scaled Jacobian | 0.0131 | 0.0179 | +0.0051 |
| FE iterations | 262 | 259 | −4 |
| Total runtime (s) | 2.498 | 12.018 | +9.505 |
| FE success | 100% | 100% | No observed difference |

Historical poor-element percentage increased for the GNN arm in all 23 clean
and all 23 moderate cases; only 3 of 23 poor-tier cases had a lower percentage
than the classical arm. Historical surface-distance and volume-error summaries
were unchanged to the reported precision in the archived CSV. Minimum scaled Jacobian
improved on many historical cases while the aggregate poor-element fraction
worsened, illustrating that a single worst-element metric and the tail count
can move in different directions.

These are **historical results from an earlier method configuration**, not a
direct benchmark of the consolidated current GNN. They are shown as
development history and a useful negative result. Because the same test IDs
were used in the archived benchmark, this test cohort has already informed
prior development decisions and cannot be described as untouched for a new
confirmatory analysis.

### 3.2 Current exploratory validation evaluation

The current V3 exploratory CSV contains 63 successful rows: three validation
phantom IDs (`0009`, `0061`, and `0147`), all three tiers, and seven methods.
The GNN used threshold 0.2, selected as the least-bad observed candidate by
median minimum scaled Jacobian. The three IDs are all included in the 20-mesh
calibration-search sample. Therefore, this evaluation is re-use of
calibration/validation observations, not an independent validation set.

Paired medians for the current exploratory GNN versus classical comparison:

| Tier | Classical poor elements (%) | GNN poor elements (%) | Classical min. SJ | GNN min. SJ | Classical FE iterations | GNN FE iterations | Classical runtime (s) | GNN runtime (s) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Clean | 12.456 | 5.785 | 0.01453 | 0.00897 | 255 | 247 | 2.980 | 15.500 |
| Moderate | 13.550 | 6.002 | 0.01317 | 0.00571 | 250 | 248 | 3.304 | 18.247 |
| Poor | 14.823 | 6.079 | 0.01142 | 0.00323 | 245 | 237 | 3.848 | 23.376 |

Across the nine phantom-tier cases, the GNN arm had median poor-element
percentage 6.002% versus 13.550% for classical, a paired median change of
−7.529 percentage points. The poor-element percentage was lower in all nine
cases. However, median minimum scaled Jacobian fell from 0.01255 to 0.00571;
it was lower in seven of nine paired cases. Median first-percentile scaled
Jacobian increased from 0.1162 to 0.1523, and median mean scaled Jacobian
increased from 0.4195 to 0.4268. Thus, broad lower-tail quality improved in
this small sample while the single worst element became worse in most cases.

The median GNN modified-element fraction was approximately 95.4%, with a
median of 53,523 modified elements per mesh. This is not a sparse/local
correction in terms of affected element count. All nine GNN meshes remained
watertight, had zero inverted elements, and passed the implemented FE success
check; classical had the same 100% FE success in this small sample. The median
FE iteration count was 247 for GNN versus 255 for classical. Since every arm
had successful FE outcomes, the binary success measure was at ceiling and did
not discriminate the methods.

Median 95th-percentile Hausdorff distance was 0.011420 for GNN and 0.011454
for classical, a small descriptive difference. Signed volume error medians
were −1.516% and −1.517%, respectively, indicating similar volume bias. The
median total runtime was 18.247 seconds for GNN compared with 3.496 seconds
for classical, approximately 5.22 times the classical runtime. The GNN run
also took longer than the rule-targeted median runtime (18.247 versus 14.885
seconds) while modifying a similarly large fraction of elements.

No inferential p-values are reported for the current three-phantom sample.
The nine phantom-tier rows are repeated measurements on only three independent
phantoms, and the same phantoms participated in threshold selection.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Main findings

Three findings are supported by the available artifacts.

First, the synthetic data and mesh-processing workflow is reproducible and
produces complete per-stage artifacts, including clean reference surfaces,
three controlled degradation levels, classical meshes, graph caches, and
validation/test ID lists.

Second, the classifier predicts correction-derived labels well on validation
graphs. Recall is particularly high, consistent with finding almost all
positively labeled elements. But the current data show that prediction
accuracy does not by itself determine whether the downstream intervention is
safe, selective, or computationally useful.

Third, correction outcomes are metric-dependent. In the small current
exploratory sample, the GNN arm sharply reduced the fraction of elements below
the poor-quality cutoff and improved the first percentile and mean scaled
Jacobian. At the same time, its worst minimum scaled Jacobian deteriorated in
most paired meshes, the modification fraction exceeded that of the
rule-targeted baseline, and runtime was several times higher than classical.
The strict calibration gates correctly rejected this operating point.

The archived benchmark tells a different historical story: the legacy GNN
arm often improved minimum scaled Jacobian but increased poor-element
percentage on clean and moderate cases. This demonstrates why conclusions
must be tied to a versioned operator and a clearly defined outcome. It is not
valid to pool the historical test results with the current V3 validation
sample or to call the contrast a controlled before-and-after improvement.

### 4.2 Why good prediction metrics did not produce an eligible correction

Several mechanisms are plausible and are consistent with the observed
measurements, but they have not yet been isolated experimentally:

1. **Prediction task and operational objective differ.** The classifier is
   trained to identify elements whose scaled Jacobian improves under a
   label-generation correction. Calibration instead imposes constraints on
   mesh-level median minimum quality and modification fraction. A strong
   element-level F1/AP score does not optimize those global constraints.
2. **High recall can broaden the intervention.** Validation recall is 0.998
   at the training threshold. Predictions are evaluated in a poor-element
   neighborhood, and flagged element vertices become candidate vertices.
   The observed median modified fraction near 95% indicates that the
   effective intervention is broad, regardless of the initial local
   prediction region.
3. **The local acceptance objective does not enforce the calibration's global
   minimum-quality constraint.** The penalty-based objective can trade a
   small number of very poor elements against improvements in a larger
   neighborhood. Its local floor is not the same as a global mesh-level
   minimum-SJ acceptance test. The observed drop in minimum SJ alongside
   reduced poor-element percentage is consistent with that distinction.
4. **The selected threshold is not a safety guarantee.** The best observed
   candidate among four thresholds was still ineligible. Threshold 0.2 was
   selected only for an explicitly exploratory demonstration and must not be
   described as having passed calibration.
5. **Optimization targets may be misaligned with solver readiness.** The
   current FE check had 100% success across all methods in the three-phantom
   sample, so it could not show a success-rate advantage. Iteration-count
   differences were modest and descriptive.
6. **The computational cost is material.** The current GNN correction
   required a median 18.25 seconds per mesh versus 3.50 seconds for the
   classical path. A large modified region and serial quality-guard checks
   may explain much of this cost, but profiling is needed before attributing
   it to a particular implementation stage.

These hypotheses should be tested separately; none alone is established as
the causal explanation for the failed calibration.

### 4.3 Strengths

- **Controlled ground truth:** every degraded input has a clean procedural
  reference surface.
- **Tiered stress testing:** blur, noise, and threshold shifts create
  reproducible input-quality conditions.
- **Instance-level split:** all tiers of one phantom stay together, reducing
  direct geometry leakage across splits.
- **Deterministic geometric labels:** element features and labels do not
  require manual annotation.
- **Shared operator across targeted arms:** rule and GNN targeting use a
  common configured smoother, helping separate targeting from operator
  choice.
- **Paired evaluation:** methods start from the same classical input mesh.
- **Broad outcome reporting:** the pipeline records element quality,
  geometry, volume, watertightness, FE outcomes, and runtime.
- **Negative results are visible:** failed calibration is persisted rather
  than silently replaced by a default threshold.
- **Useful development artifacts:** previews and per-stage logs make it
  possible to inspect where data quality and topology change.

### 4.4 Limitations and threats to validity

1. **Synthetic-to-real domain gap.** Procedural phantoms do not model the full
   intensity statistics, artifacts, anatomy, acquisition protocols, and
   segmentation errors of clinical CT. No clinical generalization claim is
   warranted.
2. **Calibration and demonstration overlap.** The three validation IDs used
   in the exploratory final CSV (`0009`, `0061`, `0147`) also appear in the
   threshold-search sample. The demonstrated result is consequently subject
   to selection/reuse bias.
3. **Small current demonstration cohort.** There are only three independent
   phantom IDs. Nine phantom-tier rows are repeated observations, not nine
   independent phantoms. No inferential test is appropriate for this
   demonstration.
4. **Historical test-set reuse.** The archived 23-ID benchmark uses exactly
   the same IDs now designated as the test split. Those outcomes have already
   been used in prior development/inspection. The cohort cannot be represented
   as untouched for a new confirmatory claim.
5. **Protocol/version drift.** The legacy benchmark used method arms and
   correction variants that were later removed or consolidated. The legacy
   benchmark CSV does not encode enough label-version/calibration metadata to
   prove equivalence to the current operator.
6. **Labels reflect the chosen smoother.** The classifier learns whether
   elements improve under a specific `smart_smooth` configuration. It is not
   a general mesh-quality repair oracle and may not transfer to another
   smoother, mesher, element order, or physical domain.
7. **Metric trade-offs.** A lower poor-element fraction can coexist with a
   worse single minimum scaled Jacobian. Reporting only one summary can
   conceal a potentially important worst-element regression.
8. **FE proxy limitations and ceiling effect.** The custom linear-tet
   heat/Poisson assembly check is not a complete physical simulation. In the
   small current sample, all methods succeeded, so the binary outcome had a
   ceiling effect.
9. **Runtime environment dependence.** Timings were collected on a local
   system and include the implementation's current routines. They should not
   be compared across machines without hardware/software descriptions and
   repeated measurements.
10. **Uncertainty in the 100-operation calibration estimate.** Its 20-mesh
    stratified subset is a speed compromise. Passing on that subset would not
    establish that a threshold passes on the full validation cohort.
11. **No independent current-method test result.** The current final test
    evaluator has not produced a confirmatory result under a qualified
    operating configuration.

### 4.5 Recommended amendments

The following changes are research recommendations, not changes already
validated by this study.

#### A. Make the optimization objective match the safety gate

- Use a lexicographic or constrained objective: preserve positive volumes
  first, prevent any unacceptable global minimum-SJ reduction, then reduce
  poor-element count.
- Add a mesh-level trust-region/line-search check after each sweep. Reject or
  reduce an update if it worsens the global minimum or a pre-registered
  lower-tail metric beyond a tolerance.
- Compare the current local penalty with alternatives that penalize new poor
  elements and worst-element regression explicitly.
- Report the number and identity of elements newly made poor, not only the
  net count.

#### B. Improve targeting and efficiency

- Measure and constrain the fraction of elements whose incident vertices
  actually move. Distinguish flagged, candidate, and modified elements in all
  reports.
- Test tighter probability thresholds and smaller neighborhood expansions
  only within a new validation protocol; do not select them using the test
  outcomes.
- Cache or vectorize feature and neighborhood calculations where profiling
  shows they dominate; profile each correction substage before optimizing.
- Use a staged intervention: run on a small candidate region, re-evaluate,
  and expand only if quality criteria are not met.

#### C. Align training with the operational target

- Train a ranking or policy objective that predicts the *mesh-level change*
  and risk of proposed actions, not only elementwise correctness.
- Include explicit negative examples where the smoother reduces poor-element
  count but worsens minimum quality or geometry.
- Calibrate using per-phantom summaries and constrained metrics, with a
  larger validation cohort and uncertainty intervals.
- Compare classification-only, regression-only, rule-based, and learned
  targeting under the same operator and computational budget.

#### D. Strengthen evaluation design

- Because the historical 23-phantom test IDs have already been analyzed,
  generate a new independent phantom cohort and freeze it before method
  revisions for any confirmatory evaluation.
- Perform calibration and model selection only on training/validation data;
  document all decisions before opening the new test results.
- Use the phantom as the statistical resampling unit, with tiers treated as
  repeated measures. Report paired per-phantom effects and cluster-aware
  uncertainty intervals.
- Increase the number and diversity of independent phantoms, then repeat
  across multiple training seeds.
- Evaluate on real, ethically approved, de-identified imaging only if the
  research scope and permissions support it.
- Expand the FE study to more discriminative material/load/boundary
  conditions and report conditioning or other numerical stability measures,
  while retaining the current sanity check as a limited proxy.
- Pre-register primary outcomes and safety margins, especially whether the
  primary success criterion is poor-element count, lower-tail quality,
  minimum quality, or solver behavior.

## 5. Conclusion

This project delivered a reproducible synthetic CT-to-mesh workflow, a
geometric graph representation, and a compact GraphSAGE predictor trained
from correction-derived labels. The saved model predicts those labels with
validation F1 0.885 and AP 0.903. Those prediction metrics, however, did not
translate into an eligible correction policy: all four thresholds in the
reduced calibration search modified a larger fraction of elements than the
rule-targeted baseline and failed the minimum-scaled-Jacobian requirement.

In a small, calibration-overlapping three-phantom demonstration, the
exploratory GNN threshold reduced poor-element percentage and improved mean
and first-percentile scaled Jacobian, while worsening the minimum scaled
Jacobian on most paired cases and increasing runtime by approximately fivefold
relative to classical processing. FE checks succeeded for both arms, leaving
no observed binary success-rate gain. The archived legacy benchmark also
showed mixed quality effects and used the same phantom IDs now assigned to
the test split.

The appropriate conclusion is therefore not that GNN correction improved
simulation readiness. Rather, the project establishes a working experimental
pipeline and highlights a key methodological result: strong per-element
prediction metrics are insufficient when the downstream correction objective
and mesh-level safety criteria are misaligned. A revised globally
quality-constrained correction policy and a fresh independent test cohort are
needed before a confirmatory performance claim.
